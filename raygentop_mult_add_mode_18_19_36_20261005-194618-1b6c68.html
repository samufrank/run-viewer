A stage is the logic between one register and the next. A block is one instance of the target mode. These rules hold for any synchronous design on any FPGA. Numbers that depend on this architecture are in the last section; use them, do not restate them as rules.

Must hold

Going against these produces a wrong design, not a slower one.

Observability. Only a block's output is visible. A value read by anything other than the arithmetic being taken into the block (another expression, a module output port, another module) cannot go inside a block. Keep it outside, or recompute it for those readers. Check the source; detection does not see port consumers. Operands are exempt: the block takes a copy and the original signal still exists.

Register control. A register carries its reset, enable and initial value. These modes hold no registers, so taking a register inside deletes that behaviour. If anything depends on it, keep the register and drive it from the block's result, or leave the site alone.

A bound on a port's values may be used only if the plan states it and names its source in the design, on a line of its own beginning ASSUMES:; such a rewrite is recorded as verified under that assumption. Weigh

The clock period is the delay of the slowest stage in the whole design. A stage's delay is the sum of everything on it: each block's fixed delay, each wire, each soft cell. Each relationship below names what it does to delay and to area. If a plan goes against one, say why.

1. Slowest stage → all outputs

Your task names the design's slowest stage and what is on it.

Slowest stage → delay

Relationship: A rewrite that does not touch the slowest stage cannot make the design faster, and must leave every stage no slower than it was. A rewrite that does touch it must make it shorter, not merely different. Compare every stage you change against it.

Slowest stage → area

Relationship: A rewrite that cannot shorten the clock period is paid for in tiles alone. Say so in the plan.

2. Block to register → delay

Relationship: A block's result goes to a register, or into the next block, with nothing but wire between. Any soft arithmetic between a block and its register is added to the stage.

3. Blocks in series → all outputs
Blocks in series → delay

Relationship: Each block in series adds its own delay plus one wire. Sum them with the wire in and the wire out, and compare against the slowest stage. That comparison, not a fixed count, says how many fit.

Blocks in series → area

Relationship: None. Series or side by side, the same blocks take the same tiles.

4. Arithmetic inside the block → all outputs

Arithmetic inside the block is what the mode's expression computes rather than what sits in series with it: a multiply and one addend, or two products and their sum, depending on the mode.

Arithmetic inside the block → delay

Relationship: Free. The block's delay is the mode's own and does not rise because its adder is used. This is where a conversion wins: soft logic leaves the stage and the block's delay already covers the work. An adder feeding the block's addend port is mapped into the block; an adder reading the block's result is on the path after it (rule 2). A block whose addend port is tied to a constant takes in nothing; if the multiply it replaces is already on a tile, the conversion changes the tile count and nothing else.

Arithmetic inside the block → area

Relationship: Flip-flops fall where product registers go. Do not count on LUT savings.

5. Registers → all outputs
Registers → delay

Relationship: A register may move only if no stage becomes longer and the module's latency is unchanged. If one must be added to keep latency, put it where it splits the longest stage. Every other operand entering that stage must be delayed by the same amount, or the stage adds values from different cycles.

Registers → area

Relationship: A register placed mid-chain costs operand-delay registers that a register after the chain does not.

6. Tiles → area

Relationship: A block occupies one DSP tile in one mode. Do not assume the multiply it replaces frees a tile: the synthesis tool may already have placed that multiply on a tile shared with others. Count the blocks against the tiles the unmodified design uses and the device's count, both in your task.

What the mode can do. mult_add_int_18x19 is out = ax*ay + bx: exactly ONE product plus one addend, with bx registered at the operand edge (not at the sum edge) and chainin taking only another block's chainout or a constant. So the only fusions it can absorb are (a) a product whose addend is available one full cycle before the product's own operands are registered, or (b) a product whose sibling partial sum arrives on a chain pin with the cycle alignment the chain gives. Neither shape exists in this design.

Group A — the nine matmult products (raygentop.v:335$15, 336$16, 337$17, 338$18, 339$19, 340$20, 341$21, 342$22, 343$23). Left: the original truncates each product before the sum.
raygentop.v:335-343 register the full 32-bit products (am11 <= Ax*m11, …), and raygentop.v:348-350 sum them after slicing each one: Cx <= am11[30:15] + am12[30:15] + am13[30:15]. Dropping bits 14:0 of each product before adding is not the same value as adding first and slicing after — the discarded low halves carry into bit 15. That is the "original truncates each product before the sum → no" row of the arithmetic table, so the three-product reduction behind Cx/Cy/Cz cannot be fused at all, by one block or by a chain.

A degenerate 1:1 swap of a single matmult multiply (bx=0, chainin=0, truncation left in soft logic) is also ruled out, on latency: around that multiply the original has exactly one register stage (the am register), the block has two (operands then result). The only register on the same path that could be pulled in is the Cx register, and it cannot move before the truncation-and-add that must stay outside the block, so the block's result would land on the cycle Cx is already due and push the module's latency from 2 to 3. That is the "fewer stages" row, with neither escape available → leave the site alone.

Group B — the nine bilinearintrp products (raygentop.v:2874$348 … 2882$356). Left: no addend exists at the block's operand edge, and a bx=0 swap is pure tile cost.

Operand registers (ul,vl,wl,rul,…, raygentop.v:2863-2872) and product registers are at consecutive edges, so the block's two stages would line up with the module's 2-cycle latency.

What kills it is the mode's arity. r is a three-product reduction; the block holds one product and one addend. To put i2r/i3r on bx they would have to be valid before the edge that registers ul/rul — but vl,rvl,wl,rwl are themselves only registered at that same edge, so no partial sum exists that early.
